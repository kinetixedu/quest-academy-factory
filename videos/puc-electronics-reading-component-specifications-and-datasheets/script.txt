H1|0.35|Aarav builds a circuit with exactly the right resistor value. A few seconds later, the resistor is too hot to touch, and it smells burnt.
H2|0.5|The value was correct. So what went wrong?
I1|0.35|The value is only one number. Every part also has limits, called its specifications.
I2|0.65|They are printed in a document from the maker, called a datasheet.
S1|0.35|A datasheet has a few main sections.
S2|0.35|First, a short description and the key features.
S3|0.35|Then the absolute maximum ratings. These are limits you must never cross.
S4|0.35|Then the electrical characteristics, with minimum, typical and maximum values.
S5|0.65|Then graphs, and finally the size and shape of the package, so you know it fits your board.
K1|0.35|Which numbers matter most? For a resistor, the resistance, the tolerance and the power rating.
K2|0.35|For a capacitor, the capacitance, the tolerance and the voltage rating.
K3|0.35|For an inductor, the inductance and the largest current it can carry.
K4|0.65|Tolerance tells you how far the real value can be from the printed one. A one kilo ohm resistor with five percent tolerance can be anywhere from nine hundred and fifty to one thousand and fifty ohms.
B1|0.35|Now let's test Aarav's resistor on the breadboard. It is one kilo ohm, and its power rating is a quarter of a watt.
B2|0.35|Turn up the supply. The current rises, and the power rises even faster, because it depends on the voltage squared.
B3|0.35|At ten volts, the power is zero point one watts. That is well inside the rating. The resistor stays cool.
B4|0.35|At twenty volts, the power is zero point four watts. That is above the rating.
B5|0.65|The resistor overheats. That is exactly what happened to Aarav. The value was right, but the rating was too small.
T1|0.8|The value tells you what a part is. The rating tells you what it can survive.
D1|0.35|So what is the most a resistor can take? Let's work it out.
D2|0.7|Power is voltage times current. P equals V times I.
D3|0.7|From Ohm's law, I equals V by R. So P equals V squared by R. Or, in terms of current, I squared R.
D4|0.7|Now set P equal to the rating, and solve. The largest safe voltage is the square root of P times R.
D5|0.65|And the largest safe current is the square root of P by R. P in watts, R in ohms.
W1|0.35|Worked example. One kilo ohm, rated a quarter of a watt.
W2|0.6|V max equals the square root of zero point two five times one thousand. That is about fifteen point eight volts.
W3|0.65|I max equals the square root of zero point two five by one thousand. About fifteen point eight milliamperes. Twenty volts was too much.
G1|0.35|One more thing. A rating holds only up to a stated temperature.
G2|0.35|In this example datasheet, the full quarter watt is allowed up to seventy degrees.
G3|0.65|Above that, the allowed power falls in a straight line, down to zero at one hundred and fifty five degrees. This is called derating.
X1|0.35|Now, the exam trap.
X2|0.35|Many students think the absolute maximum rating is a safe value to work at. It is not.
X3|0.65|It is the limit where damage can begin. Always design inside the recommended operating values, with room to spare.
X4|0.65|Where is this asked? In PUC board papers. List the specifications of a resistor or a capacitor, or work out a power rating or a tolerance range.
Q1|5.4|Try this. A four hundred and seventy ohm resistor is rated half a watt. What is the largest current it can safely carry?
A1|0.35|C. About thirty two point six milliamperes.
A2|0.35|Use I max equals the square root of P by R.
A3|0.35|That is the square root of zero point five divided by four hundred and seventy.
A4|0.65|The square root of zero point zero zero one zero six. About zero point zero three two six amperes, or thirty two point six milliamperes.
