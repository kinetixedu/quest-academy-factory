B2|0.35|A variable supply, an ammeter, and one resistor. A voltmeter sits across the resistor.
B3|0.35|Set the supply to two volts. The current is zero point five amperes.

L1|0.35|This is Ohm's law. Georg Simon Ohm, a German physicist, published it in eighteen twenty seven.
L2|0.7|At a steady temperature, the current through a conductor is directly proportional to the voltage across it.
L3|0.7|So V is proportional to I. Put in a constant, R, and V equals I times R.
L4|0.7|Turn it around, and R equals V divided by I.
L5|0.65|Resistance is measured in ohms, written with the Greek letter omega. One ohm is one volt per ampere.
L6|0.65|Metals obey this law at a steady temperature. Some parts, like a diode, do not. We call them non ohmic.
T1|0.8|Double the push, double the flow. Resistance stays the same.
G1|0.35|So what decides the resistance of a wire? Three things.
G2|0.7|Length. A longer wire means more bumps. R is proportional to the length, L.
G3|0.7|Thickness. A wider wire gives more paths. R is inversely proportional to the area of cross section, A.
G4|0.7|And the material. Put them together. R equals rho times L, divided by A.
G5|0.65|Rho is the resistivity of the material, in ohm metres. Copper has a very low resistivity, so wires are made of copper.
G6|0.65|That is why the long, thin cable charges slowly. More length, less area, more resistance.
W1|0.35|Worked example. A resistor has six volts across it, and a current of zero point three amperes.
W2|0.35|R equals V by I. Six divided by zero point three is twenty ohms.
