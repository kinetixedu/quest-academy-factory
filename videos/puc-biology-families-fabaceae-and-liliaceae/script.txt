Z1|0.35|Let's zoom into a pea flower, one part at a time.
Z2|0.5|The flower is bisexual and zygomorphic. It splits into equal halves in only one plane. Its five sepals are joined.
Z3|0.5|It has five free petals, shaped like a butterfly. One standard at the back, two wings at the sides, and two in front that form the keel. This is vexillary aestivation.
Z4|0.5|Inside the keel are ten stamens, in two bundles. Nine are joined together, and one is free. This is called diadelphous.
Z5|0.65|In the centre is one carpel. The ovary is superior, with one room and many ovules. It becomes a pod, called a legume.
P1|0.35|Now the monocot family, Liliaceae, the lily family. Let's build its flower, ring by ring.
P2|0.75|First, the plant. Perennial herbs that live on through underground bulbs, corms or rhizomes. The leaves sit mostly at the base. They are long and narrow, with parallel veins.
P3|0.75|Step one. The flower is bisexual and actinomorphic. Sepals and petals look alike, so we call them tepals. There are six, in two rings of three, often joined into a tube.
P4|0.75|Step two. Six stamens, three plus three, fixed on the tepals. They are epitepalous.
P5|0.75|Step three. Three fused carpels in a superior ovary. It has three rooms, with many ovules on the central axis. That is axile placentation.
P6|0.75|Step four. The fruit is a capsule, rarely a berry, and the seeds keep their endosperm.
V1|0.75|Side by side. Symmetry. A pea flower is zygomorphic. A lily flower is actinomorphic.
V2|0.75|Petals. Fabaceae has five petals: a standard, two wings and a keel. Liliaceae has six tepals, in two rings of three.
V3|0.75|Stamens. Fabaceae has ten, nine joined plus one free. Liliaceae has six, fixed on the tepals.
V4|0.75|Ovary. Fabaceae has one carpel and one room. Liliaceae has three fused carpels and three rooms.
