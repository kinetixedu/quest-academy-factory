P1|0.35|First, one new word. Phase.
P2|0.4|A phase is one even part of a mixture. One solid, one liquid, or one gas.
P3|0.4|Ice floating in water has two phases. Air is one phase, even with many gases in it.
P4|0.6|An equilibrium with more than one phase is called heterogeneous. Hetero means different.
S1|0.35|Let's put a closed jar of water on our molecule bench.
S2|0.4|Fast molecules escape from the surface into the air above. That is evaporation.
S3|0.4|Some vapour molecules hit the water and stick back. That is condensation.
S4|0.4|Soon both rates are equal. The vapour pressure stops changing.
S5|0.4|Now take out half the water. The vapour pressure stays exactly the same.
S6|0.6|So K here is just the pressure of the water vapour. K p equals p of water.
L1|0.35|Why does the liquid drop out? Think of a chocolate bar.
L2|0.4|Cut it in half. Each piece is smaller, but packed just as tightly.
L3|0.65|For a pure solid or a pure liquid, the packing is its concentration, moles per litre. It never changes, so we leave it out of K.
D1|0.35|Let's see it with the lime kiln, step by step.
D2|0.75|Step one. Solid calcium carbonate breaks into solid calcium oxide and carbon dioxide gas.
D3|0.75|Step two. Write K the normal way. Calcium oxide times carbon dioxide, over calcium carbonate.
D4|0.75|Step three. Both solids have fixed concentrations. We fold them into the constant.
D5|0.75|Step four. K c equals the carbon dioxide concentration. And K p equals the pressure of carbon dioxide.
G1|0.35|Now three more examples.
G2|0.4|Solid nickel and carbon monoxide gas make a nickel gas, used to purify nickel. The solid nickel drops out.
G3|0.4|Silver oxide with nitric acid. The solid and the liquid water drop out. Only the dissolved substances stay.
G4|0.4|Carbon dioxide with solid carbon gives carbon monoxide. Only the two gases stay.
G5|0.6|The rule. Keep gases and dissolved substances. Drop pure solids and pure liquids.

W1|0.3|Now, one worked example.
