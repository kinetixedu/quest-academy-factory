H5|0.6|No. Two shortcuts do it. The theorems of perpendicular and parallel axes.
P1|0.35|Note: these theorems are not in the current NCERT book. They are in the ISC, JEE and NEET syllabus.
P2|0.4|The perpendicular axes theorem works only for flat bodies, like a coin or a plate.
P3|0.6|The parallel axes theorem works for any body, as long as one axis passes through the centre of mass.
G1|0.35|First, perpendicular axes. Lay the coin flat. Draw x and y in its plane, and z straight up through its centre.
G2|0.4|A bit of the coin sits at x and y. Its distance from the z axis is r, and r squared equals x squared plus y squared.
G3|0.4|Multiply by m and add every bit. The sum of m r squared splits in two.
G4|0.6|Those are I about x, and I about y. So I z equals I x plus I y.
G5|0.6|For the coin, I z is half M R squared, and by symmetry I x equals I y. So each is a quarter M R squared: the coin spun on its edge.
G6|3.1|Now you try. A ring has I equal to M R squared about its central axis. About a diameter, is it M R squared, or half? Pause and decide.
G7|0.6|Half M R squared! The two diameters share the total equally.
S1|0.35|Now parallel axes, on our motion notebook. A two kilogram disc, radius point two metres, spinning about its centre. I is point zero four.
S2|0.4|Move the axis point one metres away from the centre. Now the centre of the disc goes round in a circle too.
S3|0.35|Slow-mo replay. Freeze.
S4|0.4|The motion has two parts. The centre goes round the axis, like a mass M at distance d. That adds M d squared.
S5|0.6|And it still turns about its own centre, keeping I c m. Total: point zero six.
S6|0.4|Slide the axis to the rim. d is point two, M d squared is point zero eight, and I is point one two. Three times as much.
S7|0.6|On the graph, I against d squared is a straight line. It starts at I c m, and its slope is the mass.
D1|0.35|Why does this work? Step by step.
D2|0.75|Step one. Measure each bit from the centre of mass with r. From the new axis, its distance is r plus d.
D3|0.75|Step two. Square it and add. You get the sum of m r squared, plus two d times the sum of m r, plus M d squared.
D4|0.75|Step three. Measured from the centre of mass, the sum of m r is zero. So the middle part vanishes.
D5|0.75|Step four. What is left is I c m plus M d squared.
T1|0.7|Remember this. Parallel axes: I equals I c m plus M d squared. Perpendicular axes: I z equals I x plus I y, for flat bodies only.
K1|0.4|Side by side. A thin rod about its middle has M L squared over twelve.
K2|0.6|Shift the axis to its end, half a length away. Add M times L over two, squared. That is M L squared over three, four times bigger. That is why the ruler was hard to spin by its end.
K3|3.2|Now you try. Of all the parallel axes through a body, which one gives the smallest moment of inertia? Pause and decide.
K4|0.65|The one through the centre of mass! Any other axis adds M d squared on top.
W1|0.35|Now, one worked example.
W2|0.5|A two kilogram disc of radius point two metres. Find I about a tangent in its plane.
W3|0.75|First, perpendicular axes. About a diameter, I is half of point zero four. Point zero two.
W4|0.75|The tangent is parallel to it, a distance R away. Add M R squared: two times point zero four. Point zero eight.
W5|0.65|So I is point zero two plus point zero eight. Point one kilogram metre squared.
X1|0.35|Now, the exam trap.
X2|0.4|Many students add M d squared between any two parallel axes.
X3|0.65|But one axis must pass through the centre of mass. Between two other axes, go through the centre of mass first.
E1|0.6|Where is this asked? In finding I about an edge, a corner or a tangent, using one or both theorems.
