L1|0.35|Now let's watch the cambium ring form, step by step.
L2|0.75|Step one. In a young dicot stem, a strip of cambium sits in each vascular bundle, between xylem and phloem. The intrafascicular cambium.
L3|0.75|Step two. Medullary ray cells next to these strips start dividing too. They form the interfascicular cambium, between the bundles.
L4|0.75|Step three. The strips join up into one complete ring, the cambial ring.
L5|0.75|Step four. The ring cuts off cells on both sides. More inside, as secondary xylem. Fewer outside, as secondary phloem.
L6|0.75|Step five. The older phloem is pushed outward and slowly crushed. The primary xylem stays near the centre.
T1|0.6|Remember this. The cambium adds xylem inside and phloem outside.
C1|0.35|So where do the rings come from?
C2|0.45|In spring, the cambium is very active. It makes many cells with wide vessels. This light wood is spring wood, or early wood.
C3|0.45|In winter, the cambium slows down. It makes fewer cells, with narrow vessels. This darker, denser wood is autumn wood, or late wood.
C4|0.65|One light band and one dark band together make one annual ring. Count the rings, and you can estimate the age of the tree.
D1|0.35|Inside a big trunk, the wood is not all one colour.
D2|0.45|The dark centre is heartwood. Its dead cells are filled with tannins, resins, oils and gums. It no longer carries water, but it is hard, strong and resists insects and microbes.
D3|0.45|The paler outer wood is sapwood. It carries water and minerals from root to leaf.
D4|0.65|So the heartwood holds the tree up, and the sapwood keeps the water flowing.
