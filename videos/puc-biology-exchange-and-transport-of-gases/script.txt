H4|0.5|Here is the short answer. Gases move by diffusion, from high pressure to low. Blood carries oxygen mostly on haemoglobin, and carbon dioxide mostly as bicarbonate.
H5|0.65|By the end, you will know partial pressure, the diffusion membrane, gas transport and the oxygen dissociation curve.
B1|0.35|First, a new word. Partial pressure.
B2|0.45|Air is a mix of gases. The part of the pressure that comes from one gas is its partial pressure. We write p O 2 for oxygen, and p C O 2 for carbon dioxide.
B3|0.65|Gases diffuse from where their partial pressure is high to where it is low. Carbon dioxide also dissolves twenty to twenty-five times better than oxygen.
Z1|0.35|Let's zoom into the place of exchange.
Z2|0.5|Here is one alveolus, wrapped in a net of tiny blood vessels called capillaries.
Z3|0.5|Zoom to where the alveolus touches a capillary. Only a very thin wall separates the air from the blood.
Z4|0.65|Zoom in. This is the diffusion membrane, with three layers: the thin squamous epithelium of the alveolus, the endothelium of the capillary, and the basement substance between them. Together, less than a millimetre thick.
L1|0.35|Now follow one oxygen molecule, step by step.
L2|0.75|Step one. In the alveolus, p O 2 is high, one hundred and four millimetres of mercury.
L3|0.75|Step two. The blood arriving has low p O 2, only forty. So oxygen diffuses into the blood.
L4|0.75|Step three. In a red blood cell, oxygen binds to haemoglobin and forms oxyhaemoglobin. Each haemoglobin can carry up to four oxygen molecules.
L5|0.75|Step four. Blood leaving the lungs has a p O 2 of ninety-five. It flows to the tissues, where p O 2 is only forty.
L6|0.75|Step five. Oxygen leaves the haemoglobin and diffuses into the cells.
T1|0.6|Remember this. Gases always move from high partial pressure to low.
C1|0.35|Now the most famous graph in this chapter.
C2|0.45|Plot p O 2 against the percent saturation of haemoglobin with oxygen. The curve is sigmoid, shaped like the letter S. This is the oxygen dissociation curve.
C3|0.65|In the alveoli, high p O 2, low p C O 2, fewer hydrogen ions and a lower temperature help oxygen bind. In the tissues, low p O 2, high p C O 2, more hydrogen ions and a higher temperature make oxygen let go.
D1|0.35|Now compare how blood carries the two gases.
D2|0.45|Oxygen. About ninety-seven percent rides in red blood cells, on haemoglobin. Only three percent is dissolved in plasma, the liquid part of blood.
D3|0.65|Carbon dioxide. Twenty to twenty-five percent rides on haemoglobin as carbamino-haemoglobin. About seventy percent travels as bicarbonate. About seven percent is dissolved in plasma.
U1|0.35|How does carbon dioxide become bicarbonate?
U2|0.45|Red blood cells are full of an enzyme called carbonic anhydrase. At the tissues, it joins carbon dioxide and water into carbonic acid, which splits into bicarbonate and hydrogen ions.
U3|0.6|At the lungs, the same reaction runs backwards, and carbon dioxide is set free to be breathed out. Every hundred millilitres of blood delivers about five millilitres of oxygen to the tissues, and four of carbon dioxide to the alveoli.
U4|3.2|Your turn. In the tissues, p C O 2 is high. Does that help haemoglobin hold oxygen, or let it go? Pause and decide.
U5|0.65|It lets go! High p C O 2 makes oxygen leave haemoglobin, right where the cells need it.
W1|0.35|Now a worked example. Which way does each gas move at the alveolus?
W2|0.45|Oxygen. Alveolus one hundred and four, blood arriving forty. The difference is sixty-four, so oxygen moves into the blood.
W3|0.45|Carbon dioxide. Blood arriving forty-five, alveolus forty. The difference is only five, so carbon dioxide moves out into the alveolus.
W4|0.65|Why is a gap of five enough? Carbon dioxide dissolves twenty to twenty-five times better, so it diffuses fast even with a small push.
