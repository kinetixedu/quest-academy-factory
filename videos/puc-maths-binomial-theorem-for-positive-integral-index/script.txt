D1|0.35|First, two small words.
D2|0.4|A binomial is an expression with two parts, like a plus b. Bi means two.
D3|0.6|The power tells you how many brackets to multiply. a plus b, to the power three, means three brackets side by side.
P1|0.4|Now watch the brackets multiply. From each bracket, you pick one part: a or b.
P2|0.4|Pick a, a, a, and you get a cubed. Pick a, a, b, and you get a squared b.
P3|0.4|Every different pick makes one piece. Three brackets, two choices each. That makes eight picks.
P4|0.65|Now sort the pieces. One pick gives a cubed. Three picks give a squared b. Three give a b squared. One gives b cubed.
P5|0.65|So a plus b, cubed, is a cubed, plus three a squared b, plus three a b squared, plus b cubed.
C1|0.4|Here is the key question. Why are there three picks that give a squared b?
C2|0.4|Because you only decide which bracket gives the b. It can be the first, the second, or the third.
C3|0.75|Choosing r things out of n is exactly n C r, from the combinations episode. Here, three C one is three.
C4|0.65|So every number in front of a term is an n C r. It simply counts the ways to choose the brackets that give b.
L1|0.4|Now let the power grow. Watch n count up from one to ten.
L2|0.4|Each new power adds one more term. The numbers in front grow fast, and they always rise and fall evenly, like a hill.
L3|0.6|At n equals ten, they are one, ten, forty five, one hundred twenty, two hundred ten, two hundred fifty two, and then back down.
L4|0.65|Add them all, and you get one thousand and twenty four. That is two to the power ten.
G1|0.35|Now we can write the theorem in one line.
G2|0.75|Step one. The first term is a to the power n. Its number is n C zero, which is one.
G3|0.75|Step two. Each next term swaps one a for one b. The power of a goes down by one. The power of b goes up by one.
G4|0.75|Step three. In front of a to the n minus r, times b to the r, sits n C r. That is the number of ways to choose r brackets for b.
G5|0.75|Step four. The last term is b to the power n, with n C n, which is also one.
G6|0.65|So there are n plus one terms. And in every term, the two powers add up to n.
S1|0.4|Side by side. Many people think a plus b, squared, is just a squared plus b squared.

S3|0.6|So a plus b, squared, is a squared, plus two a b, plus b squared. The middle term is what the shortcut forgets.
S4|3.2|Now you try. Is a plus b, cubed, equal to a cubed plus b cubed? Pause and decide.
S5|0.65|No! The middle terms, three a squared b and three a b squared, are missing. Only the first and the last terms match.
T1|0.7|Remember this. Pick one part from every bracket, and n C r counts the ways.
V1|0.35|Two quick tricks come straight out of the theorem.
