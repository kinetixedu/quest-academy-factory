H5|0.65|By the end, you will know each phylum by its key feature and its NCERT examples.
S1|0.35|Let's open the jars.
P1|0.5|One. Porifera, the sponges. Cellular level. Water flows in through the ostia and out through the osculum, kept moving by collar cells. Examples: Sycon, Spongilla and Euspongia.
P2|0.5|Two. Coelenterata, also called Cnidaria. Radial and diploblastic, with stinging cells called cnidoblasts, and two body forms, the polyp and the medusa. Examples: Physalia, Adamsia and Meandrina.
P3|0.5|Three. Ctenophora, the comb jellies. They swim with eight rows of ciliated comb plates, and they can glow. Examples: Pleurobrachia and Ctenoplana.
P4|0.5|Four. Platyhelminthes, the flatworms. Flattened from top to bottom, acoelomate, with flame cells for excretion. Examples: Taenia, the tapeworm, and Fasciola, the liver fluke.
P5|0.5|Five. Aschelminthes, the roundworms. Round in cross-section and pseudocoelomate, with separate males and females. Examples: Ascaris, Wuchereria and Ancylostoma.
P6|0.5|Six. Annelida. Metameric and coelomate, with closed circulation and nephridia for excretion. Examples: Nereis, Pheretima, the earthworm, and Hirudinaria, the leech.
P7|0.5|Seven. Arthropoda, the largest phylum. A chitin exoskeleton and jointed appendages. Examples: Apis, the honey bee, Anopheles, the mosquito, and Limulus, the king crab, a living fossil.
P8|0.5|Eight. Mollusca, the second largest phylum. A soft body with a muscular foot, often inside a shell, and a file-like rasping organ, the radula. Examples: Pila, Pinctada, Sepia and Octopus.
P9|0.5|Nine. Echinodermata, the spiny-skinned animals. All marine, with a water vascular system for moving and feeding. Examples: Asterias, the starfish, Echinus and Ophiura.
P10|0.65|Ten. Hemichordata. A worm-like body in three parts, proboscis, collar and trunk, with a rod in the collar called the stomochord. Examples: Balanoglossus and Saccoglossus.
K1|0.35|Now the key: a few yes or no questions.
K2|0.45|Cells only, with no tissues? Porifera. Two germ layers? Stinging cells mean Coelenterata. Comb plates mean Ctenophora.
K3|0.45|Three germ layers but no body cavity? Platyhelminthes. A false cavity, a pseudocoelom? Aschelminthes.
K4|0.45|A true coelom and a segmented body? Annelida. Add jointed legs, and it is Arthropoda.
K5|0.65|A true coelom with no segments? A water vascular system means Echinodermata. A muscular foot means Mollusca. Proboscis, collar and trunk mean Hemichordata.
T1|0.6|Remember this. Every non-chordate phylum has one feature that gives it away.
U1|0.35|Two pairs get mixed up the most.
U2|0.45|Coelenterates and ctenophores are both radial and diploblastic. But coelenterates carry stinging cnidoblasts, and ctenophores swim with comb plates.
U3|0.6|Flatworms and annelids are both worm-like. But flatworms are flat, unsegmented and acoelomate, while annelids are segmented, with a true coelom.
U4|3.2|Your turn. A leech sucks blood, like many parasitic flatworms. Is it a flatworm, or an annelid? Pause and decide.
U5|0.65|Annelid! Hirudinaria, the leech, is segmented and has a true coelom, just like the earthworm.
W1|0.35|Now, a worked example: a jar labelled cuttlefish. Is it a fish?
W2|0.45|Tissues and organs, not loose cells. Not Porifera.
W3|0.45|Three germ layers and a true coelom. Not a flatworm or a roundworm.
W4|0.45|No segments, no water vascular system, and no notochord. So it is not a chordate, and not a fish.
W5|0.65|A soft body, a muscular foot, and a radula. It is Sepia, a mollusc.
X1|0.35|Now, the exam trap.
X2|0.4|Many students place Hemichordata under the chordates, because of the stomochord in its collar.
X3|0.65|But the stomochord only looks like a notochord. Hemichordata was once a sub-phylum of Chordata, and is now a separate non-chordate phylum.
