B1|0.35|First, the one rule behind every breath.
B2|0.45|Air always flows from higher pressure to lower pressure. Pressure is how hard the air pushes.
B3|0.65|When the pressure inside the lungs is lower than the air outside, air flows in. This is inspiration. When it is higher, air flows out. This is expiration.
Z1|0.35|Let's zoom into the chest to find the muscles that do the work.
Z2|0.5|The lungs sit in the air-tight thoracic chamber. Below it is the diaphragm, a dome-shaped sheet of muscle.
Z3|0.5|Zoom closer. Between the ribs are more muscles, the intercostal muscles. Inter means between, and costal means ribs.
Z4|0.65|Zoom to the ribs. When the external intercostal muscles contract, they lift the ribs and the sternum up and out.
L1|0.35|Now follow one breath in, step by step.
L2|0.75|Step one. The diaphragm contracts and flattens. The chest gets more room, from front to back.
L3|0.75|Step two. The external intercostal muscles contract. They lift the ribs and sternum, so the chest gets bigger the other way too.
L4|0.75|Step three. The lungs stretch with the chest, so their volume goes up.
L5|0.75|Step four. The pressure inside the lungs drops below the air pressure outside.
L6|0.75|Step five. Air rushes in until the pressures are equal. That is inspiration.
T1|0.6|Remember this. Bigger chest, lower pressure, air flows in.
C1|0.35|Now breathe out.
C2|0.45|The diaphragm and the intercostal muscles relax. The diaphragm and the sternum go back to their normal places, and the chest gets smaller.
C3|0.65|The lungs shrink, the pressure inside rises above the outside, and air is pushed out. When we breathe hard, muscles in the abdomen add extra force.
D1|0.35|Let's put the two side by side.
D2|0.45|Inspiration. The diaphragm and external intercostal muscles contract. Chest volume goes up, lung pressure goes down, and air comes in.
D3|0.65|Expiration. The muscles relax. Chest volume goes down, lung pressure goes up, and air goes out. A healthy person breathes twelve to sixteen times a minute.
U1|0.35|Now, how much air?
U2|0.45|A spirometer is a machine that measures the volume of air breathed in and out. Doctors use it to check how well the lungs work.
U3|0.6|One normal breath is the tidal volume, about five hundred millilitres. The extra you can force in is the inspiratory reserve volume, two and a half to three litres. The extra you can force out is the expiratory reserve volume, about one litre. The air that always stays behind, even after the hardest breath out, is the residual volume, about one point one to one point two litres.
U4|3.2|Your turn. Can the air of the residual volume ever reach the spirometer? Pause and decide.
U5|0.65|No! Residual volume never leaves the lungs, so a spirometer cannot catch it.
W1|0.35|Now, a worked example. Capacities are just volumes added together. Take TV five hundred, IRV two thousand five hundred, ERV one thousand, and RV one thousand one hundred millilitres.
W2|0.45|Vital capacity is the most air you can breathe out after the deepest breath in. It is ERV plus TV plus IRV. That is four thousand millilitres.
W3|0.45|Functional residual capacity is the air left in the lungs after a normal breath out. It is ERV plus RV. That is two thousand one hundred.
W4|0.65|Total lung capacity is all the air the lungs hold after the deepest breath in. It is vital capacity plus RV. That is five thousand one hundred millilitres.
