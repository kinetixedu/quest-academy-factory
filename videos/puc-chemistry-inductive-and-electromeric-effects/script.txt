P1|0.35|First, a quick reminder. In a bond, two atoms share a pair of electrons, like two kids holding one rope.
P2|0.35|If one atom is more electronegative, it pulls the pair closer. It gets a small minus charge, called delta minus.
P3|0.35|The other atom gets delta plus. Now that atom pulls on its own neighbour a little.
P4|0.35|This pull passed along a chain of sigma bonds is called the inductive effect.
P5|0.65|It is permanent. It is always there, even when the molecule is just sitting in a bottle.
S1|0.35|Let's watch it on our molecule bench. Here is a chain of three carbons with a chlorine atom at the end.
S2|0.4|Chlorine pulls the shared pair of the carbon chlorine bond towards itself. Chlorine becomes delta minus, carbon one becomes delta plus.
S3|0.4|Carbon one now pulls a little on the bond to carbon two. Carbon two becomes double delta plus, a smaller charge.
S4|0.4|Carbon three feels even less. Triple delta plus. The pull fades fast, like the paper clips.
S5|0.4|After about three bonds, the effect is almost zero.
S6|0.4|Groups that pull electrons show the minus I effect. Halogens, nitro, cyano, carboxy, ester and aryloxy groups.
S7|0.65|Groups that push electrons show the plus I effect. Alkyl groups like methyl and ethyl are plus I groups.
D1|0.35|Now back to Diya's vinegar. Why does chlorine make the acid stronger? Step by step.
D2|0.75|Step one. An acid is strong if it gives away its H plus easily. What is left behind is a negative ion.
D3|0.75|Step two. In chloroacetic acid, the chlorine pulls electrons through the chain, away from that negative charge.
D4|0.75|Step three. The negative charge gets spread out, like a heavy bag shared by friends. A spread out charge is more stable.
D5|0.75|Step four. A more stable ion forms more easily, so the acid gives H plus more easily. It is a stronger acid.
D6|0.65|More chlorines pull harder. So trichloroacetic acid is stronger still. And a plus I group like methyl does the opposite. It makes an acid weaker.
L1|0.35|Now the second effect. Take a molecule with a double bond. A double bond has a sigma pair and a pi pair.
L2|0.4|When an attacking reagent comes near, the whole pi pair shifts to one atom. This is the electromeric effect.
L3|0.4|Watch. An H plus comes near ethene. The curved arrow starts at the pi pair and ends on the carbon where H plus joins. This is the plus E effect.
L4|0.4|Now a cyanide ion comes near a C O double bond. The pi pair moves away from the attacked carbon, onto the oxygen. This is the minus E effect.
L5|0.65|Take the reagent away, and the pi pair goes back. The electromeric effect is temporary. When it acts against the inductive effect, the electromeric effect wins.
B1|0.35|Compare them side by side. One is always on. The other switches on only when a reagent arrives.
B2|3.2|Pause and think. You remove the attacking reagent. Which effect is still there?
B3|0.65|The inductive effect! It is permanent. The electromeric effect vanishes once the reagent leaves.
T1|0.7|Remember this. Inductive is a pull that never stops. Electromeric switches on only for a reagent.
