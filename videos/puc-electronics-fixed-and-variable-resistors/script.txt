H2|0.5|What is behind that knob?
R1|0.35|A resistor. A part that opposes the flow of current.
R2|0.35|Its resistance is measured in ohms. More ohms means less current for the same voltage.
R3|0.65|There are two big families. Fixed resistors, whose value never changes. And variable resistors, whose value you can change.
F1|0.35|Fixed resistors come in a few kinds.
F2|0.35|Carbon composition resistors are made of carbon powder mixed with a binder. They are cheap, but not very precise.
F3|0.35|Carbon film and metal film resistors have a thin layer coated on a ceramic rod. Metal film is the more precise and stable one.
F4|0.65|Wire wound resistors use a long wire, often nichrome, wound on a ceramic core. They can handle large power without burning out.
V1|0.35|Now the variable ones.
V2|0.35|A potentiometer has three terminals. Two ends of a resistive track, and a sliding contact called the wiper.
V3|0.35|A rheostat uses only two terminals, one end and the wiper, to change the current.
V4|0.65|A preset, or trimmer, is a tiny one. It is set once with a screwdriver, and then left alone.
B1|0.35|Let's build the volume control on the breadboard. A potentiometer, with the music signal across its two ends.
B2|0.35|The wiper picks off part of the signal and sends it to the speaker.
B3|0.35|Watch the scope. Channel one is the full input. Channel two is what reaches the speaker.
B4|0.35|Turn the knob up. The wiper slides, and the output wave grows. The music gets louder.
B5|0.65|Turn it down, and the output shrinks. At the bottom end, it is silent.
S1|0.8|Fixed resistors set a value. Variable resistors let you choose it.
D1|0.35|So how much does the wiper pick off? Let's work it out.
D2|0.7|The resistance of a uniform track grows with its length. So R is proportional to L.
D3|0.7|If the wiper sits a length x from the bottom, the lower part has a resistance R x equals x over L, times R.
D4|0.7|The current through the whole track is I equals V in over R.
D5|0.7|The output is that current times the lower part. So V out equals V in times R x over R.
D6|0.65|This is called a voltage divider. Volts in volts, ohms in ohms, and the fraction has no unit.
W1|0.35|Worked example. A ten kilo ohm potentiometer is connected across nine volts.
W2|0.35|The wiper is at thirty percent of the track, so R x is three kilo ohms.
W3|0.6|The current is nine divided by ten thousand. That is zero point nine milliamperes.
W4|0.65|V out is nine times three over ten. Two point seven volts.
X1|0.35|Now, the exam trap.
X2|0.35|Many students think a potentiometer and a rheostat are connected the same way. They are not.
X3|0.65|A potentiometer uses all three terminals and divides the voltage. A rheostat uses two terminals and changes the current.
X4|0.65|Where is this asked? In PUC board papers. Classify resistors, describe a wire wound resistor, or tell a potentiometer from a rheostat.
