B1|0.35|First, meet the cell envelope.
B2|0.45|Most prokaryotic cells, especially bacteria, have a cell envelope with three layers, tightly bound together.
B3|0.65|From outside to inside: the glycocalyx, then the cell wall, then the plasma membrane. Each layer has its own job, but together they act as one protective unit.
Z1|0.35|Let's zoom into one bacterium.
Z2|0.5|The whole bacterium. On its surface you can see a long flagellum, and many small hairs, called pili and fimbriae.
Z3|0.5|Zoom into the edge. Here are the three layers of the envelope, the glycocalyx, the cell wall and the plasma membrane.
Z4|0.5|Zoom again, into the cytoplasm. Here are the ribosomes, tiny bodies that make proteins. Each one is about fifteen by twenty nanometres. A nanometre is a thousand times smaller than a micrometre.
Z5|0.65|Each ribosome has two parts, a large fifty S part and a small thirty S part. Together, they form a seventy S ribosome.
L1|0.35|Now the three layers, step by step.
L2|0.75|Step one. The glycocalyx is the outermost layer. In some bacteria it is a loose sheath, called the slime layer.
L3|0.75|Step two. In other bacteria, the glycocalyx is thick and tough. Then it is called the capsule.
L4|0.75|Step three. The cell wall gives the cell its shape. It gives strong support, so the bacterium does not burst or collapse.
L5|0.75|Step four. The plasma membrane is semi permeable. It lets some things through and not others, and it is built much like ours.
L6|0.75|Step five. The envelope decides the Gram stain. Bacteria that take up the Gram stain are Gram positive. Those that do not are Gram negative.
T1|0.6|Remember this. Glycocalyx, cell wall, plasma membrane, from outside in.
C1|0.35|Now a special part of the membrane, the mesosome.
C2|0.45|The mesosome is formed when the plasma membrane folds into the cell. These folds can be vesicles, tubules or lamellae, which are flat sheets.
C3|0.45|The mesosome helps in making the cell wall, in copying the DNA, and in sharing the DNA between the two daughter cells.
C4|0.65|It also helps in respiration and secretion, and it gives the membrane more surface area and more enzymes. In cyanobacteria, other folds called chromatophores hold pigments.
D1|0.35|Now the parts that stick out from the surface.
D2|0.45|Some bacteria can swim. They move with thin threads, called flagella. A flagellum has three parts: a filament, a hook and a basal body. The filament is the longest part.
D3|0.45|Pili and fimbriae are surface hairs too, but they do not help in moving. Pili are long tubes made of a special protein.
D4|0.65|Fimbriae are small, bristle like fibres. In some bacteria, they help the cell stick to rocks in streams, and to the tissues of a host.
U1|0.35|Back inside the bacterium. Its ribosomes are attached to the plasma membrane.
U2|0.45|Several ribosomes can sit on one messenger RNA, like beads on a thread. This chain is a polysome, and its ribosomes read the message to make proteins.
U3|0.6|The cell also stores food and minerals in inclusion bodies, with no membrane around them. Examples are phosphate granules, cyanophycean granules and glycogen granules. Some photosynthetic bacteria also have gas vacuoles.
