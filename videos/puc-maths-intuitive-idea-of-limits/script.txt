H5|0.65|She never lands on it, yet the gap gets as close to zero as you like. That idea has a name. It is called a limit.
P1|0.35|So what is a limit?
P2|0.4|A limit asks one question. When the input gets closer and closer to a number, what does the output get close to?
P3|0.4|We write it like this. The limit, as x tends to a, of f of x, equals l.
P4|0.4|x tends to a means x creeps towards a, as close as we like. But x never has to equal a.
P5|0.65|And l is the value that f of x settles near. For Diya, the moves keep going and the gap tends to zero.
L1|0.35|Now watch a real function do this. Take h of x equals x squared minus four, over x minus two.
L2|0.4|Try x equals two. The top is zero and the bottom is zero. Zero over zero has no value, so h of two does not exist.
L3|0.4|So we creep up on two instead. From the left: one point nine, then one point nine nine, then one point nine nine nine.
L4|0.4|h gives three point nine, then three point nine nine, then three point nine nine nine. It is closing in on four.
L5|0.65|From the right: two point one, two point zero one, two point zero zero one. h gives four point one, four point zero one, four point zero zero one. Also closing in on four.
D1|0.35|Why four? Here is the reason, in small steps.
D2|0.75|Step one. The top, x squared minus four, splits into x minus two, times x plus two.
D3|0.75|Step two. While x is not two, x minus two is not zero. So we can cancel it, top and bottom.
D4|0.75|Step three. What is left is just x plus two. A straight line, with one tiny hole at x equals two.
D5|0.65|Step four. As x tends to two, x plus two tends to four. So the limit is four, even though h of two does not exist.
S1|0.4|Side by side. The value at a point is what the function gives exactly at that point. Here, there is none.
S2|0.6|The limit is what the function gets close to near that point. Here, it is four. A limit looks near a, never at a.
S3|3.2|Now you try. Can a function have a limit at a point where it has no value at all? Pause and decide.
S4|0.65|Yes! h has no value at two, but its limit there is four. The hole does not stop the limit.
R1|0.4|Notice we came from two sides. Coming from numbers just below a gives the left hand limit.
R2|0.4|Coming from numbers just above a gives the right hand limit.
R3|0.6|The rule from NCERT: the limit exists only when the left hand limit and the right hand limit are the same number.
R4|3.2|Test it. The greatest integer function, square bracket x, at x equals one. Does the limit exist? Pause and think.
R5|0.65|No! Just below one, like zero point nine nine, it gives zero. Just above one, it gives one. Zero and one do not match, so there is no limit.
T1|0.7|Remember this. A limit looks near the point, from both sides, and both sides must agree.
W1|0.35|Now, one worked example.

W4|0.75|Step two. From the right, f of x is three x minus two. As x tends to two, it tends to six minus two, four.
W5|0.75|Step three. Both sides give four. So the limit exists, and it equals four.
W6|0.75|Step four. f of two is seven, but that does not matter. The limit never looks at the point itself.
