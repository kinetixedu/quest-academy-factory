H1|0.35|Aarav turns the dial of his meter. A V with a straight line. A V with a wavy line. Numbers like two, twenty and two hundred.
H2|0.5|What do all these markings mean?
Y1|0.35|Every meter speaks in symbols. Let's learn them.
Y2|0.35|A V with a straight line and a dashed line means D C volts. A steady voltage, like a battery.
Y3|0.35|A V with a wavy line means A C volts. A voltage that keeps changing direction, like the supply at home.
Y4|0.35|A stands for amperes, the current. The Greek letter omega stands for ohms, the resistance.
Y5|0.35|Now the sockets. The black lead always goes into the socket marked COM, which means common.
Y6|0.35|The red lead goes into the socket for volts and ohms. Large currents often get a separate socket of their own.
Y7|0.65|In circuit diagrams, a voltmeter is a circle with a V, an ammeter is a circle with an A, and a galvanometer is a circle with a G.
G1|0.35|Now, ranges. A range is the largest value the meter can show on that setting.
G2|0.35|Here is a nine volt battery. On the two volt range, the meter cannot show nine. The display says O L, which means over the limit.
G3|0.35|Switch to the twenty volt range. Now it reads nine point zero zero.
G4|0.65|On the two hundred volt range, it reads nine point zero. Same voltage, but one digit is lost. So pick the lowest range that is still above the value.
G5|0.35|The oscilloscope has ranges too. Its volts per division knob is a range switch.
G6|0.35|At one volt per division, the nine volt line shoots off the top of the screen.
G7|0.65|Turn the knob to five volts per division. Now the line sits at one point eight divisions, safely on the screen.
G8|0.65|Its other controls are simple. Intensity sets the brightness, focus makes the trace sharp, and the position knobs move it up, down, left or right.
P1|0.35|An analogue meter has a needle and a scale. Here is how to read it.
P2|0.7|First, note where the needle stops, counted in scale divisions.
P3|0.7|Divide by the total divisions on the scale. That is the fraction of full scale.
P4|0.7|Multiply by the range. That is your reading.
P5|0.65|The same needle position on a bigger range means a bigger value. So always check the range before you read.
E1|0.35|Worked example. A scale has fifty divisions. On the ten volt range, the needle stops at thirty two.
E2|0.6|The reading is thirty two over fifty, times ten.
E3|0.65|That is zero point six four times ten, which is six point four volts.
K1|0.35|Now, the precautions.
K2|0.35|One. Start on the highest range, then step down. A big value on a small range can damage the meter.
K3|0.35|Two. For D C, watch the polarity. Red to plus, black to minus. Reverse it, and an analogue needle kicks the wrong way.
K4|0.35|Three. Measure resistance only with the power off. For an analogue ohmmeter, touch the probes together and set the needle to zero first.
K5|0.35|Four. Read the needle with your eye straight in front of it. Looking from the side gives a parallax error.
K6|0.65|Five. When you finish, turn the dial to off, so the cell inside does not drain.
S1|0.8|Highest range first. Then step down.
T1|0.35|Now, the exam trap.
T2|0.35|Many students think O L on the display means the meter is broken. It does not.
T3|0.65|O L means the value is over the range. Switch to a higher range. On the ohms setting, it means the resistance is too high to show, like an open circuit.
T4|0.65|Where is this asked? In PUC board papers. Name the symbols, list the precautions, and read a value from a scale and a range.
Q1|5.6|Try this. An analogue voltmeter has a fifty division scale. On the ten volt range, the needle stops at thirty five. The meter is switched to the twenty five volt range. Where will the needle stop now?
A1|0.35|C. Fourteen divisions.
A2|0.35|First, find the voltage. Thirty five over fifty, times ten, is seven volts.
