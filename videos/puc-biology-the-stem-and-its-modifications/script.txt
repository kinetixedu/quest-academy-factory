D1|0.45|First, where does a stem come from? Inside every seed sits a tiny baby shoot, called the plumule. It grows up into the stem.
D2|0.6|A stem has nodes: the points where leaves grow. The part of the stem between two nodes is called an internode.
D3|0.6|A stem also carries buds. A bud at the tip is a terminal bud. A bud in the axil, the angle between a leaf and the stem, is an axillary bud.
D4|0.6|The stem holds out the branches, leaves, flowers and fruits. It carries water, minerals and food through the plant.
D5|0.65|Some stems also store food, give support or protection, or make new plants. For those jobs, they change their shape.

P1|0.5|Now let's meet stems in disguise, one job at a time.
P2|0.75|Storing food. Potato, ginger, turmeric, zaminkand and Colocasia are underground stems that store food.
P3|0.75|They also help the plant survive a bad season, like a dry summer, and grow again later. This is called perennation.
P4|0.75|Climbing. In gourds like cucumber and pumpkin, and in grapevines, axillary buds grow into stem tendrils: thin, coiled springs that wrap around a support.
P5|0.75|Protection. In Citrus and Bougainvillea, axillary buds turn into thorns: woody, straight and pointed. They keep grazing animals away.
P6|0.75|Making food. In dry places, Opuntia has a flat green stem, and Euphorbia a fleshy round one. They have chlorophyll, so they make food by photosynthesis.
P7|0.75|Spreading. In mint and jasmine, a thin side branch grows for a while, then arches down to touch the ground, and roots there.
P8|0.75|Pistia and Eichhornia float on water. Their short side branch carries a rosette of leaves and a tuft of roots, ready to become a new plant.
