D3|0.45|Later, Flemming saw a material inside it that took up basic dyes and turned coloured. He named it chromatin.
D4|0.65|Chromatin is made of DNA, some basic proteins called histones, some non-histone proteins, and RNA.
Z1|0.35|Let's zoom into a cell, one level at a time.
Z2|0.5|Most cells have one nucleus. In a cell that is not dividing, it holds long fine threads of chromatin, a jelly called the nuclear matrix, and one or more round bodies called nucleoli.
Z3|0.5|A nucleolus has no membrane around it. It is where ribosomal RNA is made. Cells that make a lot of protein have bigger and more nucleoli.
Z4|0.5|Zoom into the edge. The nuclear envelope is two membranes, with a gap between them called the perinuclear space, ten to fifty nanometres wide. The outer membrane often joins the ER, and carries ribosomes.
Z5|0.65|Zoom into a pore. In many places, the two membranes join and leave tiny openings, called nuclear pores. RNA and protein molecules pass through them, in both directions.
P1|0.35|Now watch the thread get packed, one step at a time.
P2|0.75|Step one. In a resting cell, chromatin is spread out, as long fine threads.
P3|0.75|Step two. When the cell gets ready to divide, each thread starts to coil.
P4|0.75|Step three. The coils fold again and again. The thread becomes short and thick.
P5|0.75|Step four. Now we can see chromosomes. Chromosomes are visible only in dividing cells.

C1|0.35|Now look at one chromosome closely.
C2|0.5|Every chromosome has a primary constriction, a narrow waist, called the centromere.
C3|0.5|On the sides of the centromere sit disc-shaped structures, called kinetochores.
C4|0.5|The centromere's position gives four types. In the middle: metacentric, two equal arms. A little off the middle: sub-metacentric, one shorter arm and one longer arm.
C5|0.5|Close to one end: acrocentric, one extremely short arm and one very long arm. Right at the end: telocentric.
