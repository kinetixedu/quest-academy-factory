Z1|0.35|Let's zoom into the skin of a leaf.
Z2|0.5|The epidermis is usually one layer of cells. Each cell has a big vacuole, and a thin lining of cytoplasm.
Z3|0.5|On the outside sits the cuticle, a thick waxy layer that stops water loss. That is the shine. Roots have no cuticle.
Z4|0.5|Zoom closer. Here is a stoma, a tiny pore. Two bean-shaped guard cells surround it. They have chloroplasts, and they open and close the pore.
Z5|0.5|Some cells beside them become subsidiary cells. The pore, the guard cells and the subsidiary cells together make the stomatal apparatus.
Z6|0.65|In grasses, the guard cells are dumb-bell shaped. And on the stem, the skin grows hairs called trichomes, which help prevent water loss.
P1|0.35|Now the pipes. A vascular bundle can be built in four ways.
P2|0.75|Radial. Xylem and phloem sit on different radii, taking turns around the centre. Roots are built like this.
P3|0.75|Conjoint. Xylem and phloem sit together on the same radius, with phloem usually on the outer side. Stems and leaves are built like this.
P4|0.75|Open. In dicot stems, a strip called cambium lies between phloem and xylem. Cambium can make new xylem and phloem later.
P5|0.75|Closed. In monocots, there is no cambium. So the bundle cannot make new xylem and phloem.
P6|0.75|And the filling around the bundles? In stems and roots, the ground tissue forms the cortex, pericycle, pith and medullary rays. In leaves, it is the mesophyll.
C1|0.35|Now the hardest pair to tell apart: open and closed bundles.
C2|0.45|Both are conjoint. Both have xylem and phloem. Both carry water and food.
C3|0.65|But an open bundle has cambium between its phloem and xylem. A closed bundle has none.
